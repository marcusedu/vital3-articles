# Work With What You Have, Know What to Do, and Be the One Who Does It

There is a daily struggle against what keeps me from achieving what I defined myself. There are many obstacles. Three stand out.

The first two have similar weight. The third sustains the other two — and is the one that hurts most to look at directly.

Maybe you are in the same place.

---

## 1. The crutch of only marching when feeling "good"

I am not a classic perfectionist. The crutch is different: I only authorize myself to move forward when my internal state is positive.

Headache, stomachache, fatigue, a bad night's sleep, sadness from something external or internal, frustrated expectations, loss. Or the opposite: energy, a good response, rest, some victory. Life mixes these all the time. The problem is I almost exclusively marched on "good" days. On the others, I waited for it to pass.

When I moved forward without feeling good, it was almost always an urgency: a blown deadline, a major loss if I didn't act, someone important needing it immediately, or an adrenaline spike. Aside from that, the implicit rule was this: **if I am not well, it won't be done well** — and I will still feel the resistance of the bad feeling.

The fact is different. Often, when you are not well and start anyway, the state changes later. It is not the end of the world. You are not well enough to walk, and you start walking. You are not well enough for the conversation, and the conversation happens. The two-minute advice holds true: **fake it or do it for two minutes**.

The conclusion is simple and harsh: **do the best you can with what you have right now**. If what you have is sadness, do your best with the sadness. If today's maximum is three, deliver the three. If it's six, the six. If it's ten, the ten. Do not wait to be better, ready, or motivated. Do it.

There is an exception: the extraordinary. A mother dying, an accident, someone needing you right now — including yourself, in a real breakdown. That is not the everyday. A headache, boredom, a day that starts bad and improves, or the reverse: that is the mundane day. And even in an emergency, the criteria remains the same: **the best possible with what you have**. Sometimes the best is to rest and wait for it to pass.

Without this first move, the other two points become just plans on paper.

---

## 2. Minimal organization — not millimeter precision

It is not about slicing life into five-minute blocks.

Waking up at five, brushing teeth at five-oh-five, coffee at five-fifteen is pathetic and steals the time that should go toward execution.

**Minimal organization is something else.** Looking at the week and putting on the calendar only what has a time, place, or deadline: the therapist, a delivery, a client meeting, a bill due on Friday. Recurring tasks that need reminders go in too — the walk, for example. The rest is a to-do list for the next day, executed from highest to lowest priority. The plan can change. That is fine.

Last year, during driver's ed law classes, the school handed out the weekly schedule. With nothing on the calendar, in a month I barely did what I needed to do — and even wasted an extra hour on a subject I had already finished. I pulled the transcript, saw what was missing, threw the schedule into the Mac calendar, and let the notifications ring. In a little over a week, I finished what the whole month hadn't. **I knew what to do and, just as importantly, what not to do.**

The logical order with the previous point is this: even if you can already do your best with the day's state, if you don't know what that day demands, the effort scatters. You invent tasks, repeat what was already done, escape to what is easy. The minimal schedule points to the target. The daily list executes. A reminder to "pay the bill on Friday" does not need to be "pay at exactly nine." It just needs to exist on the right day.

## 3. There is no other Marcus who will do it for me

This is the heaviest point. My therapist named it this way: I treat responsibility like a bad thing and, when I can avoid it, I do. I am not necessarily irresponsible all the time. **I avoid it when I can.**

The mechanism is this: I plan and, deep down, I hope an autopilot — or another consciousness — uses my body and my knowledge to execute. But the one who takes on the task is me. Always.

It is me on the boring, heavy, painful walk. Me in the difficult conversation. Me in the boring, repetitive, bureaucratic problem. Me dealing with the sweet that appears when I am sad, instead of using food as a crutch. Me feeling my body ache at the gym. **There is no substitute.**

The fantasies culture offers make this clear exactly because they don't exist in real life:

- **In the movie *Click*,** the universal remote pauses boredom and fast-forwards to the "goal". You press the button and life happens without you having to be present for the boring part. That button does not exist.
- **In *Severance*,** upon crossing the office door, a second consciousness takes over the body: without the memories of personal life, only with the workday tasks. The "outside me" does not suffer the work; the "inside me" does not take the work home. That split does not exist.
- **In the episode of *Rick and Morty*,** Rick builds a night version of himself. While consciousness sleeps, the body washes dishes, learns a language, trains. The day Rick wakes up with the work done. There is no night Marcus.

The most that exists is the **automatic Marcus** — the slow one, the mechanical one, the low-energy one.

Even so, it is me. A numbed consciousness, maybe, ready to wake up if something goes out of control. It is not the click, it is not the severance, it is not the night clone.

Making the decision easier for this mechanical Marcus helps: leaving clothes ready, the time set, the list visible, the path frictionless. This was already the subject of another text. **It is still me who is there.**

Tomorrow morning, a hypothetical, excited, thin Marcus, willing to do the difficult conversation and the monotonous task, will not be born. The same one writing this will wake up. The same ego, the same memory, the same body. Including the fact that anyone can see and that I avoid naming with the same force: **I am obese**. Dealing with this is dealing with boredom, pain, sadness, and the refusal of the shortcut. No one does that in their sleep.

Owning this is turning on the light on what I preferred to leave in the dark.

It hurts. It is inconvenient. It is like someone slapping your face. Without this assumption, the first two points can be perfect — the best possible in the state of the day, the minimal schedule in place — and yet the agent is missing. **I am the executor. The reins are mine. No one else is coming.**

---

## The sequence, not three random tips

The three points form a sequence:

- **Without the first,** you only work in a good mood and call that a quality criterion.
- **Without the second,** the effort from the first has no target and gets lost in invention.
- **Without the third,** the first two are playacting: the plan exists for a Marcus who never arrives.

The work, from here on, is not waiting for the right state, the perfect system, or the future self.

> **It is the self of right now, in the mundane day, with whatever emotional soup there is, on the daily list, doing what only he can do.**
